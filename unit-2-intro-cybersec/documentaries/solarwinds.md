## 1. Phishing email targeting finance
Watch for emails asking to change supplier bank details, especially with urgency attached, like an "unexpected payment" that needs handling now. Also check the sender domain (slightly off spellings are common) and whether reply-to actually matches the sender.

Don't click, don't pay. Flag it in Outlook. If you need to verify it's legit, call the supplier using the number from the actual signed contract, not the number in the email. Get the finance lead involved and open a ticket with the MSP.

If money's already gone out, call the bank immediately and request a recall, then tell finance and the CEO.

Longer term: supplier bank detail changes should require sign-off from two people, not one person acting alone.

## 2. Vishing call to HR
Red flags: a cold call asking for personal data, or one where they send a link mid-call and push you to click it immediately. Urgency is usually baked into the approach.

If this happens, hang up. Don't confirm or read out anything. Call the MSP back on their published support number, never the number the caller gave you.

Report it to the HR lead and raise a ticket. If personal data already went out, the HR lead and data protection officer need to know so they can decide whether it triggers a GDPR notification.

Policy fix: the MSP should never ask for personal data over the phone. Full stop. That kind of request goes through the ticket system, no exceptions.

## 3. Pretexting as outsourced IT MSP
Red flag: someone claiming to be from the MSP but calling from a personal phone or personal email, or asking for a password or MFA code.

Never share credentials, and never approve an MFA prompt you didn't trigger yourself. To check if it's real, look for an open ticket matching what they're describing, or call the MSP's published number.

Notify your IT contact and the MSP. If credentials were shared, change the password right away and kill active sessions, then let the MSP know.

Prevention: MFA everywhere, plus a standing rule that the MSP never asks for passwords over the phone.

## 4. Tailgating at the Vantaa office
Watch for someone without a badge, hands full "conveniently," claiming they forgot their badge, or a face nobody recognizes, especially if it happens during a busy moment when people are less likely to stop and check.

Stop them at the door politely. Don't hold it open just to be polite. Have them sign in at reception, and call whoever they claim to be visiting to confirm.

Tell reception or the office manager and security. If they did get inside, escort them out and check access logs for any sensitive areas they might have reached.

Real fix is architectural, not behavioral: badge-only turnstiles and an actual visitor sign-in process. Relying on people to notice something's off in the moment isn't a strategy.
